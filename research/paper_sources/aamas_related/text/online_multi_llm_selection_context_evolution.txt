                                                                                         Abstract
                                                     Large language models (LLMs) exhibit diverse response behaviors, costs, and
                                                     strengths, making it challenging to select the most suitable LLM for a given user
                                                     query. We study the problem of adaptive multi-LLM selection in an online setting,
                                                     where the learner interacts with users through multi-step query refinement and must
                                                     choose LLMs sequentially without access to offline datasets or model internals. A
                                                     key challenge arises from unstructured context evolution: the prompt dynamically
                                                     changes in response to previous model outputs via a black-box process, which
                                                     cannot be simulated, modeled, or learned. To address this, we propose the first con-
                                                     textual bandit framework for sequential LLM selection under unstructured prompt
                                                     dynamics. We formalize a notion of myopic regret and develop a LinUCB-based
                                                     algorithm that provably achieves sublinear regret without relying on future context
                                                     prediction. We further introduce budget-aware and positionally-aware (favoring
                                                     early-stage satisfaction) extensions to accommodate variable query costs and user
                                                     preferences for early high-quality responses. Our algorithms are theoretically
                                                     grounded and require no offline fine-tuning or dataset-specific training. Experi-
                                                     ments on diverse benchmarks demonstrate that our methods outperform existing
                                                     LLM routing strategies in both accuracy and cost-efficiency, validating the power
                                                     of contextual bandits for real-time, adaptive LLM selection. Code is available at
                                                     https://github.com/EntroShape/Online_LLM_Selection


                                         1    Introduction
                                         Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a wide range of
                                         tasks, from open-ended dialogue to complex reasoning and mathematical problem solving [1, 2, 3].
                                         However, these capabilities come with significant trade-offs: more powerful models tend to be slower
                                         and more expensive, while faster, cheaper models often lack robustness or reasoning ability [4, 5]. In
                                         practice, no single model is universally optimal across all insputs or user preferences [6].
                                         This growing heterogeneity has motivated the deployment of multi-LLM systems, where multiple
                                         LLMs with different strengths (e.g., accuracy, latency, cost) are available, and a routing or selection
                                         mechanism determines which one to use for a given user query. Such multi-model setups are increas-
                                         ingly adopted in both academic research and industrial applications, where balancing performance
                                         and efficiency is critical [7, 8]. A lightweight model like Mistral may suffice for factual lookups
                                         or shallow reasoning, while more complex tasks might require high-end models like GPT-4 [9].
                                         Selecting the right LLM at the right time can yield substantial improvements in cost-effectiveness
                                         and user satisfaction [6, 10].
                                         Existing approaches to multi-LLM routing [7, 10, 11, 12] often rely on static policies, supervised
                                         learning on offline datasets, or single-step decision-making. However, real-world user interactions
                                         are often sequential and adaptive. Users refine their queries based on prior answers, leading to

                                         Preprint.
multi-step interactions where the system can revise its LLM choices dynamically. For example, a user
might ask a factual question, follow up with a clarification request, and then pose a deeper reasoning
challenge. Each step introduces new contextual information—typically derived from the model’s
earlier responses—which should influence subsequent model selection.
To support this type of dynamic, multi-step interaction, we propose a new framework for online
multi-LLM selection under unstructured context evolution. In our setting, the system interacts with a
user over a sequence of steps: at each step, it receives a prompt context—potentially incorporating
previous model responses—and must select an LLM to generate the next reply. Crucially, the prompt
evolves over time according to a black-box transformation that may include concatenation, rewriting,
user edits, or proprietary formatting. Because this evolution process is unknown, non-differentiable,
and potentially stochastic, conventional planning-based approaches such as reinforcement learning
(RL) are ill-suited. In effect, modeling how a prompt evolves would itself require reasoning at the
level of an LLM, rendering the transition dynamics unlearnable in practice.
To address these challenges—multi-LLM decision-making, the need for online adaptation, and
unstructured context evolution—we adopt a contextual bandit framework. Rather than modeling
full interaction trajectories, we decompose the process into a sequence of myopic decisions, where
the system selects the best LLM for the current context without attempting to forecast how the
prompt will evolve. This formulation is both feasible and effective: it allows us to sidestep modeling
the unknown transition dynamics while still enabling continual improvement from user feedback.
We formalize this learning objective using myopic regret, which quantifies the loss relative to the
best possible LLM choice at each step. Building on this formulation, we develop a LinUCB-based
algorithm that maintains confidence-aware estimates of each model’s performance across varying
prompt contexts. To handle practical deployment constraints, we further extend the algorithm to
account for stochastic per-query costs and budget constraints, and introduce a heuristic that prioritizes
high-quality responses early in the interaction to reflect user positional bias. Together, our framework
offers a principled and scalable online solution for adaptive, cost-aware multi-LLM selection.
Our Contributions. We make the following key contributions:

       • We introduce the first contextual bandit framework for multi-step LLM selection under
         unstructured context evolution—a realistic and underexplored setting that captures the
         adaptive nature of interactive user–LLM interactions.
       • We propose a Greedy LinUCB algorithm for per-step LLM selection and establish a sublinear
         regret bound with respect to myopic regret. Our results show that efficient online learning is
         achievable even without modeling how prompts evolve over time.
       • We extend our method to handle stochastic cost constraints and position-sensitive utility,
         designing budget-aware and position-aware variants that improve decision quality in cost-
         limited settings and prioritize early user satisfaction in multi-turn interactions.
       • We conduct extensive experiments across diverse benchmarks, demonstrating that our
         algorithms consistently outperform existing routing methods in both accuracy and cost-
         efficiency. The results further confirm the value of context evolution and the effectiveness of
         lightweight online adaptation.

Overall, our work provides a principled and lightweight foundation for orchestrating multiple LLMs
in open-ended, multi-step environments. By using contextual bandits under unstructured prompt
evolution, we demonstrate that practical and scalable multi-LLM systems can be built without relying
on offline training or complex environment simulation.
Related Work.
Several recent works [7, 12, 13] have studied LLM selection in multi-model deployments to improve
cost-efficiency and performance. MetaLLM [7] applies LinUCB to choose an LLM for each query
based on learned reward signals derived from offline preference data. MixLLM [12] formulates LLM
routing as a linear contextual bandit problem, optimizing a weighted sum of estimated accuracy, cost,
and latency. While both methods leverage contextual bandits [14, 15], they operate in a single-step
setting and do not account for prompt evolution or multi-turn query refinement. Other approaches
rely on static or offline policies. AutoMix [11] introduces a self-verification mechanism where a
lightweight model decides whether to escalate a query to a stronger LLM, but assumes access to
a reliable verification oracle and relies on a fixed cascade. Similarly, OpenAI’s Function Calling


                                                   2
Selector [8] trains a static classifier to select between GPT variants based on supervised fine-tuning,
without adapting to online feedback or context changes. Additional recent efforts include C2MAB-V
[10], which adopts a combinatorial bandit approach for selecting LLM subsets under complex reward
formulations, and LLMSelector [6], which optimizes expert-model placement across distributed
systems. The Multi-Armed Router [16] proposes an exploration–exploitation strategy for LLM
routing but focuses on single-turn inference and assumes fixed prompt representations. A growing
body of work explores multi-turn LLM orchestration. ReAct [17] and Tree of Thoughts [18] propose
agent-style reasoning pipelines where LLMs operate over multiple steps using hand-crafted heuristics.
These approaches demonstrate promising performance but rely on predefined planning rules and lack
learnable routing policies. PromptAgent [19] automates prompt optimization using Monte Carlo Tree
Search, but requires task-specific reward functions and simulated feedback, limiting its generality.
In contrast to all of the above, our work tackles the problem of online multi-step LLM selection under
unstructured and unlearnable context evolution. We propose a contextual bandit formulation that
is lightweight, feedback-driven, and equipped with provable regret guarantees—bridging the gap
between practical black-box orchestration and theoretically grounded online learning.

Why context matters and why it is unstructured. A key design choice in our setting is to let each LLM
selection depend not only on the original user query, but also on the responses of previously selected
models. This results in an evolving prompt context, where the input to each LLM is dynamically
constructed based on prior outputs. Through empirical observations (see Section 6), we find that
incorporating this context is crucial—later LLMs often perform better when they can “see” earlier
attempts, as they may correct previous errors or build upon partial answers. This motivates our
formulation of sequential multi-LLM selection with contextual adaptation.
However, modeling how the prompt evolves across steps is inherently difficult. The new context
may be formed through a combination of LLM responses, prompt concatenation, user edits, or
system-specific transformations. We formalize this process as a context evolution function g, which
maps the current context and LLM output to the next-step prompt. Importantly, g is a black-box


                                                    3
function—often non-differentiable, stochastic, or proprietary—and cannot be explicitly modeled or
learned. In practice, predicting the next prompt would require simulating how an LLM response
changes the prompt structure, which itself may require reasoning as complex as what the LLM
performs. This complexity makes multi-step planning intractable and violates the assumptions of RL,
which depends on having a learnable or simulatable transition model. By contrast, our framework
avoids this issue by adopting a myopic view: it selects the best LLM for the current context, without
trying to predict future prompt evolution. This enables robust online decision-making even under
unstructured and unpredictable context dynamics.
Why contextual bandits are the right tool. Given the limitations of RL in this setting, we turn to
contextual bandits as a lightweight yet principled alternative. Rather than modeling full trajectories, a
contextual bandit approach selects the best LLM for each step based solely on the current prompt
context. This myopic decision-making is both feasible and robust: it avoids the need to simulate
or model g, while still adapting to evolving feedback over time. As illustrated in Figure 1, our
framework supports multi-step interaction with users: at each step, the system observes a new prompt
context, selects an LLM, receives feedback, and optionally evolves the context for the next step. We
formalize the learning objective via myopic regret, which compares the agent’s chosen LLM to the
best possible LLM for the current prompt context. This yields a theoretically tractable and practically
meaningful measure of performance, even when full trajectory optimization is infeasible.
Contextual bandits are particularly attractive in this setting because they are lightweight, data-efficient,
and robust to dynamic environments [20, 21, 22]. Unlike RL, they require no offline training or
simulator access, and they adapt easily to new user populations, model APIs, and cost structures. This
makes them well-suited for real-world multi-LLM systems, where queries are diverse, budgets vary,
and models evolve rapidly. By combining theoretical rigor with practical feasibility, contextual bandits
provide a compelling foundation for adaptive LLM routing in open-ended, real-time environments.

3   Problem Formulation
We consider an online sequential decision-making problem for adaptive multi-LLM selection, moti-
vated by real-time user interactions with LLMs. Let [K] = {1, . . . , K} denote the set of available
LLMs. Each LLM k ∈ [K] is associated with an unknown feature vector θk∗ ∈ Rd , capturing its
alignment with user preferences across different prompt contexts. A learning agent interacts with
users over T rounds. In each round t ∈ [T ], the agent receives a user query Qt and engages in a
multi-step adaptive interaction with the user, lasting at most H steps. Let xt,1 ∈ Rd denote the initial
context vector derived from Qt (e.g., an embedding or prompt representation). At each step h ∈ [H],
the agent observes context xt,h and selects an LLM at,h ∈ [K] to generate a response. Let Rt,h
denote the output of LLM at,h when invoked on context xt,h . The user provides binary feedback
rt,h ∈ {0, 1}, indicating satisfaction with the result. For analytical simplicity, we model the feedback
as a linear function of the context and the selected LLM’s parameters corrupted by noise:
                                       rt,h = ⟨xt,h , θa∗t,h ⟩ + ϵt,h ,

where ϵt,h is a zero-mean sub-Gaussian noise term. This surrogate model enables the application of
linear contextual bandit algorithms despite the discrete nature of the actual user feedback. If rt,h = 1,
the round ends. Otherwise, the agent proceeds to the next step, where the context is updated by
incorporating the previous LLM response:
                                   xt,h+1 = g(xt,h , at,h , Rt,h , rt,h ),
where g is a black-box function representing how the context evolves in response to the LLM’s output
(e.g., through concatenation, rewriting, or augmentation). Importantly, as discussed in Section 2, g
is assumed to be unstructured and unlearnable: it may be stochastic, nonstationary, or governed by
proprietary model behavior, and cannot be explicitly modeled or simulated. This interaction continues
for up to H steps or until the user is satisfied. The goal is to design an online decision policy that
maximizes the expected cumulative user satisfaction over T rounds.
Myopic regret. Due to the unstructured and unobservable nature of the context evolution function g,
multi-step planning or reinforcement learning methods that rely on modeling or simulating future
states are inapplicable. Instead, we adopt a myopic regret minimization framework, where the agent
selects each LLM based solely on the current context, without modeling future transitions. At step h

Remark. While it may seem natural to compare against the best full sequence of LLMs per query (i.e.,

each decision point, without relying on assumptions about future context evolution, making it more

4     Greedy LinUCB Algorithm
In this section, we propose a LinUCB-based greedy algorithm for sequential LLM selection. As
discussed in Section 3, since future contexts evolve via an unknown and unstructured function g,
multi-step planning is intractable. We therefore adopt a myopic strategy that focuses on optimizing
per-step feedback using contextual bandits. Specifically, we adapt the LinUCB algorithm to this
setting by maintaining a separate linear model for each LLM and selecting the arm with the highest
upper confidence bound (UCB) at each step.
As described in Algorithm 1, the greedy LinUCB algorithm maintains a regularized least-squares
model for each LLM k ∈ [K], represented by a matrix Ak and a response vector bk . At each decision
step (t, h), the algorithm computes the ridge regression estimate θ̂k = A−1    k bk and evaluates the
LinUCB index for each LLM (line 7). This index combines the predicted reward θ̂k ⊤ xt, h with
an exploration bonus proportional to the estimate’s uncertainty, promoting the selection of under-
explored LLMs. The LLM with the highest LinUCB score is chosen for querying. This approach is
crucial, as selecting solely based on the highest predicted reward (pure exploitation) risks suboptimal
decisions by neglecting parameter uncertainty. After receiving the model response Rt,h and observing

procedure is repeated at each decision point without modeling the future evolution of context.

5     Budget-Aware Algorithms under Stochastic Costs
In real-time LLM selection settings, different models incur different costs due to latency, compute, or
token-based pricing. Moreover, these costs can vary stochastically with user queries. Motivated by
this, we extend our algorithm to handle per-query random costs, where each user specifies a budget
and the learner must select LLMs adaptively within that budget while maximizing user satisfaction.

5.2   Positionally-Aware Knapsack Heuristic

The greedy budget-aware algorithm introduced earlier selects LLMs sequentially by maximizing
a cost-normalized reward score. While effective in balancing reward and cost, this approach may
overly prioritize cheap LLMs and delay the use of more capable ones until later in the interaction.
In many real-world scenarios, however, users exhibit positional bias: they prefer to receive high-
quality responses early and may disengage if early attempts fail. This motivates the need to consider
positional utility, where earlier successful responses are more valuable in practice than later ones.
To better balance early accuracy and overall cost, we propose a positionally-aware knapsack heuristic
in Algorithm 2. The key idea is to incorporate cost estimates and UCB reward scores into a dynamic
selection procedure that prioritizes early deployment of high-confidence LLMs while respecting the
per-query budget. At each step, the algorithm solves a 0-1 knapsack problem over the remaining


                                                     7
LLM candidates, using UCB scores as reward estimates and empirical costs as weights. From the
knapsack solution, it selects the arm with the highest UCB score, adds it to the candidate list, and
updates the remaining budget. This process is repeated until no feasible arms remain or the budget is
exhausted. By anchoring each decision within a budget-aware optimization, the method ensures that
strong LLMs are selected as early as possible without exceeding cost constraints.
Remark. This knapsack-based strategy explicitly targets positional utility by favoring the early use
of high-performing models, rather than deferring them due to their cost. Unlike purely greedy cost-
normalized approaches, which may over-prioritize cheap but weak models, the knapsack heuristic
balances exploration and resource allocation more effectively. While the method is heuristic and
lacks a formal regret bound, it aligns closely with practical deployment goals in interactive systems.
As demonstrated in our experiments in , the algorithm delivers higher effective utility—especially
when stronger models are costly and positional preferences matter.



6       Experiments

Baselines. We compare our algorithms against online LLM routing methods. For online, contextual
bandit approaches, MetaLLM [7] employs LinUCB to select an LLM by learning a policy that
predicts a reward (balancing accuracy and cost) based on query embeddings. MixLLM [12], another
online bandit method, routes queries by optimizing a weighted sum of predicted response quality, cost,
and an explicit latency penalty. Reported optimal trade-off parameter λ = 1.4 is used for MixLLM.
Majority Voting [23] is also considered as a baseline due to its effectiveness and simplicity.
Candidate LLMs. The LLM pool, used across all baseline comparisons, included several state-of-
the-art models (i) Deepseek-v3, a state-of-the-art open-source MoE model from China with 671 billion
total parameters; (ii) llama-4-maverick, a latest open-source Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model with
400 billion total parameters and 128 experts; (iii) phi-4, a 14-billion-parameter model from Microsoft,
designed for tasks including complex reasoning and math problem-solving; (iv) gemini-2.0-flash, a
multimodal model from Google DeepMind, suitable for rapid responses. (v) Mistral-small-3.1-24b-
instruct, an efficient instruction-following model from Mistral AI; (vi) GPT-4.1-Nano, a lightweight
variant of GPT-4.1, optimized for fast and cost-effective responses.
Datasets. Experiments were conducted on various datasets covering diverse tasks and domains. (i)
MMLU-Pro [24]: A comprehensive multiple-choice question (MCQ) benchmark covering topics
across Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) disciplines. (ii) Math-500 [25]:
A question-answering (QA) benchmark featuring mathematical problems across seven distinct
undergraduate-level subjects. (iii) GPQA [26]: A dataset consisting of challenging graduate-level
multiple-choice questions (MCQs) in physics, biology, and chemistry. For our experiments, we
converted the MCQs into open-ended questions to further increase the difficulty. (iv) AIME: A QA
benchmark consisting of problems from the American Invitational Mathematics Examination, testing
advanced mathematical reasoning. Our dataset comprised of the data from AIME1 2024 and 20252 .
Evaluation Protocol. For MMLU-Pro, accuracy was used as the evaluation metric. For the remaining
datasets, responses were evaluated using Deepseek-R1[27] as an LLM grader that provided binary
feedback (“true” or “false”) based on whether the generated answer matched the gold answer. These
feedback signals were then used to compute accuracy scores. Each dataset was randomly split into an
offline training set (20%) for initialization and an online testing set (80%) for evaluation.
Experiment Specification. For our proposed algorithms, we set a maximum cascade depth of H = 4
LLMs per query. Query embeddings were generated using the sentence [28]. Query embeddings for
all evaluated algorithms are in 384-dimensional embeddings. The exploration parameter (α) was
set to 0.675 and the regularization parameter (λ) to 0.45. The given budget for each query for both
budget-aware algorithms is randomly +/- 5% to the average cost per query of greedy LinUCB. No
any cost constarint was implemented For Greedy LinUCB algorithm.

The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed online multi-LLM selection
algorithms. As seen from (Table 1), the Positionally-Aware Knapsack heuristic achieved the highest
average accuracy of 74.84%. This represents a 16.5% improvement over the best-performing baseline
router, MixLLM (64.25%), and is also notably higher than our other proposed methods, Greedy
LinUCB (71.99%) and Budget-Aware LinUCB (69.17%). In terms of cost (Table 2), this heuristic
(1.50E-03 USD average cost) is approximately 2.27 times more economical than MixLLM (3.32E-03
USD average cost) and substantially cheaper than the Voting [23] baseline (9.34E-03 USD average
cost).
The Budget-Aware LinUCB algorithm stands out for its cost-efficiency, with an average cost of
2.97E-04 USD (Table 2). This is comparable to the cost of the MetaLLM baseline (3.36E-04 USD
average cost) and approaches the cost of the most economical individual LLM, Llama-4-Maverick
(1.15E-04 USD average cost). However, Budget-Aware LinUCB delivers a significantly higher
average accuracy of 69.17% (Table 1). This is 20.7% greater than MetaLLM (57.71% average
accuracy) and 39.6% higher than the most accurate individual LLM, Gemini-2.0-Flash (50.85%
average accuracy), while being over 3.07 times more economical than our Greedy LinUCB (8.29E-04
USD average cost) for a similar accuracy. Even compared to the Random routing baseline, which has

a low average cost (1.05E-04 USD), Budget-Aware LinUCB achieves 79.4% higher average accuracy
(69.17% vs. 39.56%) for a moderately higher, yet still very low, operational cost.
The restricted average accuracy of baseline methods, MixLLM (64.25%) and MetaLLM (57.71%),
might be attributed to their formulation of optimizing a weighted sum of estimated accuracy, cost,
and latency (MixLLM) or learned reward signals (MetaLLM) for each query independently. Both
approaches operate within a single-step decision-making paradigm; their routing logic is applied only
once per user query based on the initial context. This design inherently lacks the capability to adapt
to or leverage the context evolution that occurs in multi-turn user interactions, where subsequent
prompts are dynamically modified based on prior LLM outputs. Their myopic optimization, focused
solely on the current step’s predicted outcome, limits their effectiveness in optimizing performance
over an entire multi-step conversation sequence, which is a core characteristic of real-world user-LLM
interactions and our experimental setup.

The Positionally-Aware Knapsack heuristic is specifically designed to prioritize early user satisfaction,
reflecting a positional discount where earlier high-quality responses are more valuable. Table 3
illustrates this design’s success. The Knapsack heuristic achieves an average accuracy of 71.10%
in the very first step. This remarkable initial performance accounts for approximately 95.0% of its
total average accuracy of 74.84%. This indicates that for the vast majority of queries, the Knapsack
heuristic identifies a satisfactory LLM on its first attempt. In contrast, the other proposed methods,
while effective, distribute their accuracy accumulation over more steps. The Greedy LinUCB method,
for instance, achieves 47.89% accuracy in the first step, which is only about 71.24% of its 71.99%
overall average accuracy. Similarly, the Budget-Aware Greedy LinUCB obtains 52.39% accuracy
in its initial attempt, constituting roughly 75.7% of its total average accuracy of 69.17%. Moreover,
the Knapsack heuristic satisfies user queries in an average of just 1.58 steps, significantly fewer than
the 2.52 steps for Greedy LinUCB and 2.04 steps for Budget-Aware Greedy LinUCB. These results
underscore the effectiveness of solving a knapsack problem at each step to select high-confidence
LLMs early while respecting budget constraints. This approach aligns well with practical needs in
multi-turn interactions, where users often value prompt and accurate responses, thereby enhancing
the overall user experience.

The practical deployment of multi-LLM systems necessitates robust cost control, a challenge directly
addressed by our budget-aware algorithms. Figure 2 presents Cumulative Distribution Function
(CDF) plots of per-query costs, where curves terminating at or before the designated fixed budget

Greedy LinUCB whose cost distribution frequently extends well beyond the budget line, reflecting
its lack of explicit cost constraints and resulting in higher average operational expenses as noted
in Table 2. The Positionally-Aware Knapsack heuristic, while primarily optimizing for early user
satisfaction, also exhibits commendable budget discipline due to its cost-aware knapsack formulation,
generally keeping query costs within bounds. These distributions affirm that incorporating explicit
budget-aware mechanisms, as in Budget-aware LinUCB, is highly effective and crucial for enabling
cost-efficient LLM selection, ensuring that the system can reliably operate within predefined financial
constraints without unduly sacrificing performance.

6.1.4   Sensitivity Analysis on Budget Constraint

To assess the impact of varying budget constraints, we evaluated the performance of Budget-Aware
LinUCB and the Positionally-Aware Knapsack heuristic across a range of fixed budgets on the AIME
Dataset. As can be seen in Figure 2 , at extremely low budgets (e.g., 0.00005), both algorithms
achieve zero performance, as the budget is likely insufficient to select any LLM. As the budget
increases, the performance of both algorithms generally improves. Budget-Aware LinUCB initially
outperforms the Positionally-Aware Knapsack heuristic at tighter budget constraints (e.g., from
0.00015 to 0.001). However, as the budget increases further, the Positionally-Aware Knapsack
heuristic demonstrates superior performance, surpassing Budget-Aware LinUCB around the “Current”
operational budget (0.0021437) and achieving significantly higher performance at larger budgets
(0.0035 to 0.02). Notably, the Knapsack heuristic’s performance consistently scales with increasing
budget, reaching its peak at the highest tested budget (0.02). In contrast, Budget-Aware LinUCB’s
performance peaks around a budget of 0.005 and subsequently declines at higher budget allocations.
This suggests that while Budget-Aware LinUCB is effective under strict budget limitations, the

Positionally-Aware Knapsack heuristic offers greater performance scalability and robustness as

7   Concluding Remarks
This work presents a contextual bandit framework for online multi-LLM selection under unstructured
prompt evolution, offering a lightweight and theoretically grounded solution. While our algorithms
demonstrate strong performance across diverse benchmarks, several limitations remain. First, we
adopt a myopic decision strategy and do not model long-term interaction effects, which may limit
performance in tasks requiring deep multi-step reasoning. Second, the framework assumes access
to reliable user feedback, which may be noisy or delayed in practice. Third, we treat the LLM pool
as fixed, whereas practical deployments may involve dynamic model availability or changing costs.
Future work includes extending the framework to handle delayed and noisy feedback, incorporating
non-myopic planning under partially learnable prompt dynamics, and optimizing LLM scheduling in
dynamically evolving model pools.

B    Impact of Context

Our framework posits that leveraging evolving context—the output from previous LLM interactions—
can enhance the performance of subsequent LLM selections. To specifically investigate this, we
conducted a controlled experiment on 60 questions from the AIME dataset, comparing two strategies:
Strategy 1, using Gemini-Flash as a standalone solver, and Strategy 2, a two-step sequential approach
where Mistral-Small first attempts the problem, and its output (regardless of correctness) is then
provided as context to Gemini-Flash for a second attempt. One example for positive and negative
context impact is provided in appendix.
The results demonstrate a tangible benefit from context. Strategy 2 (Mistral → Gemini w/ Context)
achieved a 25% success rate, outperforming Strategy 1’s (Gemini-Only) 20% success rate. More
tellingly, in 8 out of the 60 questions, Gemini-Only failed, Mistral’s initial attempt also failed, but
Gemini with context from Mistral’s attempt subsequently succeeded. This directly indicates that the
contextual information, even from an initially incorrect response, can provide valuable grounding,
expose flawed reasoning paths to avoid, or offer a different starting point that enables the subsequent
LLM to reach the correct solution. This aligns with the core motivation of our work: that adaptive,
multi-step interactions where context evolves can lead to better outcomes than single-shot LLM
applications.
However, the influence of context is not universally positive. In 3 instances, the Gemini-Only
strategy succeeded, while the sequential Mistral → Gemini approach failed. This suggests that poor
or misleading initial context can sometimes derail a subsequent LLM that might have otherwise
succeeded. Such cases underscore the complexity of context interaction and highlight a potential
challenge in multi-LLM cascades. The example below demonstrates a scenario where the initial
context might have had a detrimental effect.
Overall, while the sequential strategy incurred a higher average cost per question ($0.000712 for
Strategy 2 vs. $0.000229 for Strategy 1) due to invoking two models in many cases, the net gain
in accuracy (5% percentage points, with 8 direct context-driven successes) supports the hypothesis
that context evolution is a valuable mechanism. This targeted experiment highlights the potential of
contextual information to improve problem-solving in multi-LLM systems, providing a foundational
rationale for the adaptive, context-aware selection mechanisms developed in this paper. The instances
of negative impact also motivate further research into robustly determining when and how context
should be passed or filtered. Below we have provided two examples that show the impact of context.
